Abstract

The “parameter” in a Bayesian nonparametric problem is the unknown distribution \(P\) of the observation \(X\). A Bayesian uses a prior distribution for P, and after observing X, solves the statistical inference problem by using the posterior distribution of P, which is the conditional distribution of P given X. For Bayesian nonparametrics to be successful one needs a large class of priors for which posterior distributions can be easily calculated. Unless \(X\) takes values in a finite space, the unknown distribution \(P\) varies in an infinite dimensional space. Thus one has to talk about measures in a complicated space like the space of all probability measures on a large space. This has always required a more careful attention to the attendant measure theoretic problems. A class of priors known as Dirichlet measures have been used for the distribution of a random variable X when it takes values in \(R_k\), see Freedman (1963), Fabius (1964) and Ferguson (1973). This family forms a conjugate family and possesses many pleasant properties. In this paper we give a simple and new constructive definition of Dirichlet measures and remove the restriction that the basic space should be \(R_k\). We give complete self contained proofs of the three basic results for Dirichlet measures:

  1. The Dirichlet measure is a probability measure of on the space of all probability measures,
  2. it gives probability one to the subset of discrete probability measures, and
  3. the posterior distribution is also a Dirichlet measure.

A Bayesian Analysis of Nonparametric Problems: A Synthesis of Ferguson (1973)

Executive Summary

This document synthesizes the key findings of Thomas S. Ferguson’s 1973 paper, “A Bayesian Analysis of Some Nonparametric Problems,” which introduces the Dirichlet process as a prior distribution for nonparametric Bayesian inference. The central challenge addressed is the difficulty of defining workable prior distributions over spaces of probability measures. Ferguson proposes that a suitable prior should possess two key properties: (I) a large support, ensuring it assigns probability to a wide range of possible distributions, and (II) a posterior distribution that is analytically tractable after observing data.

The paper’s seminal contribution is the introduction of the Dirichlet process prior, which successfully satisfies these often-conflicting properties. A key theorem demonstrates that if the prior distribution of a random probability measure P is a Dirichlet process with parameter α, then the posterior distribution of P given a sample is also a Dirichlet process with an updated, easily calculated parameter. This analytical tractability allows for the derivation of explicit Bayes rules for a wide array of nonparametric problems.

A significant characteristic of the Dirichlet process is that a random probability measure drawn from it is discrete with probability one. Despite this, its support is sufficiently large for many applications. Ferguson demonstrates its utility by deriving Bayes estimators for fundamental statistical quantities, including the distribution function, mean, median, quantiles, variance, and covariance. These estimators typically take the form of an intuitive weighted average, blending a prior guess with the corresponding empirical estimate from the data, where the weight shifts progressively towards the data as the sample size increases.

  1. The Challenge of Nonparametric Bayesian Inference

The application of Bayesian methods to nonparametric statistical problems has historically been challenging. The primary difficulty lies in finding a workable prior distribution on the parameter space, which in this context is a set of probability distributions on a given sample space.

The paper identifies two desirable, yet often antagonistic, properties for a prior distribution in this setting:

    1. Large Support: The support of the prior distribution should be extensive, covering a substantial portion of the space of all possible probability distributions on the sample space. This ensures the model can accommodate a wide variety of true underlying distributions.
    1. Tractable Posterior: Given a sample of observations from the true probability distribution, the resulting posterior distribution should be manageable and straightforward to analyze. This is crucial for making inferences and decisions.

The paper’s central thesis is that the Dirichlet process prior successfully achieves both of these properties, providing a powerful tool for nonparametric Bayesian analysis.

  1. The Dirichlet Process Prior

To address the aforementioned challenges, Ferguson introduces and develops the Dirichlet process, a stochastic process that generalizes the finite-dimensional Dirichlet distribution.

2.1. Definition and Core Principles

The Dirichlet process is defined as a random probability measure P on a measurable space (X, A). It is characterized by a single parameter, α, which is a non-null, finite measure on the same space.

The defining characteristic of the Dirichlet process is:

For any finite measurable partition B₁, …, Bₖ of the space X, the random vector of probabilities (P(B₁), …, P(Bₖ)) has a Dirichlet distribution with parameter vector (α(B₁), …, α(Bₖ)).

This definition builds upon the standard Dirichlet distribution, which is defined for a k-dimensional vector of probabilities based on a set of independent gamma-distributed random variables. The parameter α can be interpreted as shaping the prior belief about the distribution P, with the expected value of P(A) being E[P(A)] = α(A) / α(X).

2.2. Fundamental Properties

The Dirichlet process exhibits several key properties that make it highly suitable for nonparametric inference.

Posterior Distribution (Tractability)

The most critical property for Bayesian analysis is the conjugacy of the Dirichlet process. Theorem 1 of the paper establishes this:

If P is a Dirichlet process on (X, A) with parameter α, and X₁, …, Xₙ is a sample of size n from P, then the conditional distribution of P given the sample is also a Dirichlet process with a new parameter α + Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ δₓᵢ, where δₓ is the measure that assigns mass one to the point x.

This result ensures that the posterior distribution remains within the same family as the prior, making calculations and interpretations direct and manageable. This fulfills the second desirable property (II).

Discrete Nature of Realizations

A fundamental and perhaps counter-intuitive property of the Dirichlet process is that its realizations are discrete distributions. The paper states that a random probability measure P chosen by a Dirichlet process is, with probability one, a discrete measure. This means the probability mass is concentrated on a countable set of points.

Support of the Process (Large Support)

Despite the discrete nature of its realizations, the Dirichlet process has a large support, satisfying the first desirable property (I). Proposition 3 shows that the support of the Dirichlet process with parameter α contains the set of all probability measures Q that are absolutely continuous with respect to α. If α is σ-additive, the support is precisely the set of all σ-additive probability measures whose own support is contained within the support of α.

2.3. An Alternative Constructive Definition

The paper provides an alternative, constructive definition of the Dirichlet process that makes its discrete nature more explicit. A random probability measure P can be constructed as a weighted sum of point masses at random locations:

P(A) = Σⱼ₌₁^∞ pⱼ δᵥⱼ(A)

Here, the locations Vⱼ are independent and identically distributed random variables from a base distribution related to α, and the weights pⱼ are constructed from a sequence of independent gamma variables. This representation reinforces that the process generates discrete distributions and provides another method for its analysis.

  1. Applications in Nonparametric Estimation

The true power of the Dirichlet process lies in its application to solving a variety of nonparametric statistical decision problems. The general Bayesian framework involves finding a Bayes rule for a “no-sample” problem and then extending it to a problem with a sample of size n by simply replacing the prior parameter α with the posterior parameter α + Σδₓᵢ.

The paper provides explicit Bayes estimators under squared error loss (or other relevant loss functions) for several key statistical quantities. The space is taken to be the real line (R, B).

In these formulas:

  • F̂ₙ(t) is the empirical distribution function of the sample.
  • X̄ₙ is the sample mean.
  • \(p_n = \frac{α(R)} { (α(R) + n)}\) is a weight that reflects the confidence in the prior versus the data.

A clear pattern emerges: the Bayes estimate is a convex combination of the prior guess and the corresponding estimate from the empirical data. The weight \(p_n\) given to the prior guess \(\frac{α(R)} { (α(R) + n)}\) decreases as the sample size n increases. The term \(α(R)\) can be interpreted as a measure of faith in the prior guess, equivalent to a certain number of prior observations. As \(n \to \infty\), the Bayes estimate converges to the empirical estimate, which is a desirable property.

The paper also extends this analysis to problems such as finding tolerance regions and conducting hypothesis tests involving quantiles.
